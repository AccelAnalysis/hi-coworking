# Hi Coworking Design & Brand Guide

**Status:** Canonical public-facing design authority  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Effective:** August 23, 2026  
**Baseline:** [hi-coworking.com](https://hi-coworking.com) and the existing Hi Coworking identity in this repository

---

## 1. The governing idea

> **Hi Coworking is a place first and software second.**

Every public-facing experience should feel like entering a thoughtfully run neighborhood workspace: calm, warm, useful, local, and easy to understand.

The website and digital product should help people picture themselves in the space, choose what they need, and act with confidence. They should not expose the machinery used to operate the business.

### Primary brand line

# Big ideas. Intimate space.

This remains the strongest expression of the existing Hi Coworking idea. It is brief, memorable, and accurately communicates the contrast at the heart of the brand: meaningful work in a deliberately smaller setting.

### Customer promise

**A nearby, thoughtfully designed place to focus, meet, gather, and feel part of the local community.**

### Working brand idea

**The useful neighborhood room.**

This phrase is an internal creative anchor, not necessarily public campaign copy. It should guide decisions about the space, service, website, events, bookstore, and community presence.

---

## 2. What the existing baseline gives us

The current Hi Coworking baseline already establishes several valuable assets:

- the name **Hi Coworking**;
- the line **“Big ideas. Intimate space.”**;
- a small-scale, local, flexible workspace proposition;
- Carrollton and the surrounding community as a meaningful part of the story;
- a simple **Hi** mark that behaves like a conversation or welcome symbol;
- a dark neutral and green visual direction;
- focus, flexibility, practical amenities, events, and books as part of the experience.

These should be retained.

The design system should evolve away from the baseline’s software-like traits:

- glassmorphism and blurred panels;
- gradient background decoration;
- repeated rounded cards;
- large shadows;
- badges used as headings;
- slate/indigo/emerald utility styling without a warm physical-world counterbalance;
- navigation that mixes the public place, member tools, staff tools, and administration;
- long explanatory copy where photography or a direct action would be stronger.

The new direction is an evolution, not a rejection: **keep the recognizable Hi idea; make it feel like a real place with hospitality, character, and restraint.**

## 3. Brand position

### What Hi Coworking is

Hi Coworking is a deliberately intimate neighborhood workspace for people who need a professional place to focus, meet, and connect without committing to a traditional office.

It may include coworking seats, day passes, memberships, meeting rooms, events, a bookstore, local business community programming, and future services only when those services are actually available.

### What Hi Coworking is not

Hi Coworking is not:

- a generic office-rental portal;
- a technology platform presented as the product;
- a corporate business center;
- a sprawling national coworking chain;
- an exclusive members club that feels inaccessible;
- a coffee shop with desks;
- a dashboard full of operational states;
- a feature catalog that promises services before they exist.

### The desired feeling

A visitor should feel:

1. **Welcome:** “This is for people like me.”
2. **Calm:** “I can picture getting good work done here.”
3. **Confidence:** “I understand what is available and what it costs.”
4. **Local connection:** “This belongs to this community.”
5. **Ease:** “I know what to do next.”

### Brand attributes

| We are | We are not |
|---|---|
| Warm | Cute or overly casual |
| Focused | Sterile or silent |
| Local | Provincial or insular |
| Polished | Corporate |
| Practical | Transactional |
| Design-aware | Trend-driven |
| Social when useful | Noisy or forced |
| Confident | Loud |
| Human | Folksy or artificial |

## 4. Design benchmarks

Hi Coworking should learn from excellent coworking and hospitality brands without imitating their identities.

### The Malin

Take cues from:

- large, honest interior photography;
- editorial hierarchy;
- a clear focus on how the space feels;
- direct actions such as booking a day pass, meeting, or tour;
- location-specific storytelling;
- confidence that does not require excessive explanation.

Do not copy its visual identity, exclusivity, pricing posture, or typography.

Reference: [themalin.co](https://themalin.co/)

### Switchyards

Take cues from:

- a memorable neighborhood proposition;
- a clear single-sentence explanation of the product;
- warmth and personality;
- straightforward pricing;
- photography that makes the space feel lived in;
- treating proximity to home as a primary benefit.

Do not adopt its intentionally eccentric or retro visual language wholesale.

Reference: [switchyards.com](https://www.switchyards.com/)

### Industrious

Take cues from:

- hospitality-first service language;
- warmth, natural light, greenery, and varied work settings;
- professional confidence;
- simple product grouping;
- emphasizing a good workday rather than software features.

Do not inherit large-enterprise complexity or generic corporate scale.

Reference: [industriousoffice.com](https://www.industriousoffice.com/)

### Mindspace

Take cues from:

- boutique-hotel warmth;
- local character in each location;
- thoughtful materials and sensory detail;
- place-specific writing;
- balancing focus, comfort, and community.

Do not over-romanticize the space or hide practical information.

Reference: [mindspace.me](https://www.mindspace.me/)

### NeueHouse

Take cues from:

- cultural programming as part of membership;
- minimal, confident language;
- community expressed through people and activity rather than member counts;
- editorial presentation.

Do not adopt exclusionary language or a high-fashion tone that would make Hi feel remote from its community.

Reference: [neuehouse.com](https://www.neuehouse.com/)

### The synthesis for Hi Coworking

Hi should combine:

- **The Malin’s visual confidence**;
- **Switchyards’ neighborhood clarity**;
- **Industrious’ hospitality**;
- **Mindspace’s sense of place**;
- **NeueHouse’s cultural layer**;
- with Hi’s own smaller scale, practical accessibility, local business orientation, events, and bookstore.

## 5. Non-negotiable public-interface rules

These rules apply to every public page and every member-facing surface that a customer experiences as part of the Hi Coworking product.

### 5.1 Do not make the product look like SaaS

Public pages must not use:

- dashboard-style card grids as the default page structure;
- persistent dark application bars with role-colored links;
- glass panels, decorative blur, or floating gradient blobs;
- icons inside colored squares as a substitute for real imagery;
- multiple pill badges above every heading;
- analytics-style counters unless the number genuinely helps a customer decide;
- tabular metadata where ordinary language would work;
- role names, system states, database terms, internal IDs, or provider codes;
- “module,” “resource,” “configuration,” “entitlement,” “inventory,” “activation,” “projection,” or similar internal product language.

### 5.2 Do not over-containerize

- Do not place a card inside another card.
- Do not give every section its own rounded rectangle.
- Do not use a border, background, and shadow on the same ordinary content block.
- Use page rhythm, whitespace, photography, typography, and hairline dividers before adding a container.
- A section may have a background change, but its children should not all become separate boxes.
- Full-bleed images and edge-to-edge color fields are encouraged where they support the story.

### 5.3 Show the place before explaining the system

- Real photography should carry the first impression.
- Practical details should be visible but secondary to the experience.
- Product explanations should be short and concrete.
- A visitor should understand the page within three seconds without reading paragraphs.

### 5.4 One clear next action

Every major screen or section should have one primary action and, at most, one secondary action.

Good examples:

- **Book a day pass**
- **Choose a workspace**
- **See the meeting room**
- **View upcoming events**
- **Ask about membership**
- **Buy this book**

Avoid vague actions such as **Submit**, **Continue**, or **Learn more** when a specific label is possible.

### 5.5 Hide unavailable services

A service that is not operational should normally be hidden rather than displayed as disabled software.

Where advance awareness is useful, use ordinary language such as **Coming soon** and explain the customer benefit in one sentence. Do not display internal activation conditions.

## 6. Logo and mark

### 6.1 Core identity

The existing **Hi** mark should remain the recognizable core of the identity. Its open-corner, speech-like form supports welcome, conversation, and local connection.

Use two primary forms:

1. **Primary lockup:** Hi mark + “Coworking”
2. **Compact mark:** Hi mark alone

### 6.2 Preferred usage

- Primary: Hi Ink on Hi Paper or Warm White
- Reverse: Warm White on Hi Ink
- Secondary: Hi Forest on Hi Paper
- One color only
- Use the compact mark for favicon, map marker, social avatar, and constrained mobile spaces

### 6.3 Clear space and minimum size

- Keep clear space around the mark equal to at least half the mark’s width.
- Minimum digital size for the compact mark: **24 px**.
- Minimum digital width for the full lockup: **120 px**.
- Never allow navigation, photos, or other typography to touch the mark.

### 6.4 Do not

- stretch or rotate the mark;
- add gradients, bevels, outlines, or drop shadows;
- place the mark inside an additional rounded tile when the mark already defines its own shape;
- use the map-pin artwork as the primary logo;
- change the wordmark typeface independently on different pages;
- recolor individual letters;
- use the mark as decorative wallpaper.

### 6.5 Map marker

The Hi map marker may use the compact mark, but it should be redrawn as a clean vector using the official brand type rather than relying on embedded display text. It must remain legible at 24–40 px and should have selected, hover, and accessible high-contrast states.

## 7. Color system

The current dark neutral and green direction should be retained, but shifted from cool software colors toward warmer, physical-world colors.

### 7.1 Core palette

| Name | Token | Hex | Primary use |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Hi Ink | `--hi-ink` | `#17211B` | Primary text, dark fields, logo |
| Hi Paper | `--hi-paper` | `#F5F1E8` | Primary page background |
| Warm White | `--hi-white` | `#FFFEFA` | Forms, reading surfaces, reverse text |
| Hi Forest | `--hi-forest` | `#2F6B4F` | Primary action, links, focus |
| Hi Sage | `--hi-sage` | `#DCE5DC` | Quiet background fields |
| Hi Moss | `--hi-moss` | `#8FA997` | Supporting graphics and subtle accents |
| Hi Clay | `--hi-clay` | `#A7583B` | Events, bookstore, human warmth |
| Hi Brass | `--hi-brass` | `#D6A74B` | Small highlights and special moments |
| Hi Fog | `--hi-fog` | `#E6E3DB` | Hairlines, subtle separators |
| Muted Ink | `--hi-muted` | `#5B665F` | Secondary text |

### 7.2 Functional colors

| Name | Hex | Use |
|---|---:|---|
| Success | `#2F6B4F` | Confirmation and available states |
| Warning | `#8B641E` | Time-sensitive or cautionary messages |
| Error | `#9E3F37` | Errors and destructive actions |
| Information | `#315F72` | Rare neutral information notices |

Functional colors should not become page-brand colors.

### 7.3 Contrast guidance

Approximate contrast ratios:

- Hi Ink on Hi Paper: **14.7:1**
- Hi Forest on Hi Paper: **5.6:1**
- Hi Clay on Warm White: **5.1:1**
- Muted Ink on Hi Paper: **5.3:1**
- Hi Brass on Hi Ink: **7.5:1**

These combinations support normal text at WCAG AA contrast. Still test actual components, font weights, overlays, hover states, and photography.

### 7.4 Color rules

- Hi Paper should be the normal public background, not cool slate gray.
- Hi Ink and Hi Forest should dominate.
- Use either Clay or Brass as the accent in a composition, not both by default.
- Avoid bright emerald, indigo, cyan, and saturated product-interface colors on public pages.
- Do not use multiple colored badges to categorize ordinary content.
- Never rely on color alone to communicate status.

## 8. Typography

Typography should give Hi Coworking an editorial, place-based voice rather than the appearance of a technology product.

### 8.1 Recommended type pairing

**Display and editorial:** `Newsreader`  
**Body, navigation, controls, and forms:** `DM Sans`

Both are open-source families suitable for web use.

Fallback stacks:

```css
--hi-font-display: "Newsreader", Georgia, "Times New Roman", serif;
--hi-font-sans: "DM Sans", "Helvetica Neue", Arial, sans-serif;
```

### 8.2 Usage

- Use Newsreader for hero statements, major section headings, quotes, and selected editorial moments.
- Use DM Sans for navigation, body copy, prices, forms, buttons, schedules, and utility details.
- Do not use monospace type in customer-facing interfaces.
- Avoid all-caps headings. Sentence case is the default.
- Small labels should not use excessive tracking.
- Do not make every heading bold. Use scale, spacing, and the serif/sans contrast.

### 8.3 Type scale

| Style | Desktop | Mobile | Line height |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Display | 72–88 px | 48–56 px | 0.98–1.05 |
| H1 | 52–64 px | 40–48 px | 1.05 |
| H2 | 38–48 px | 32–38 px | 1.10 |
| H3 | 26–32 px | 24–28 px | 1.15 |
| Lead | 20–24 px | 19–21 px | 1.45 |
| Body | 16–18 px | 16–18 px | 1.55–1.65 |
| Small | 14 px | 14 px | 1.50 |
| Micro | 12–13 px | 12–13 px | 1.40 |

### 8.4 Reading width

- Long-form copy: **58–72 characters per line**.
- Maximum prose width: approximately **680 px**.
- A paragraph should usually be two to four lines on desktop.
- Break longer explanations into a title, one short paragraph, and a specific action.

## 9. Photography and video

Photography is not decoration. It is the primary evidence that Hi Coworking is a real, desirable place.

### 9.1 Image priorities

A useful image library should be approximately:

- **70% place and spatial experience** — work tables, quiet corners, meeting room, light, books, materials, arrival, exterior;
- **20% people using the space** — focused work, a genuine conversation, an event, a host welcoming someone;
- **10% details** — coffee, signage, books, local art, tactile materials, plants, notebooks.

### 9.2 Visual character

Use:

- natural light;
- warm, honest color;
- real Hi Coworking spaces and nearby context;
- people in candid activity rather than posed portraits;
- visible work surfaces and chairs so visitors can judge whether they can work there;
- a mix of wide scenes, medium environmental portraits, and close details;
- both horizontal and vertical compositions for responsive use.

Avoid:

- generic stock-office photography;
- staged handshakes;
- people pointing at empty whiteboards;
- glowing blue laptop scenes;
- photos so dark or stylized that the space cannot be evaluated;
- empty-room imagery as the only story;
- extreme wide-angle distortion;
- fake community scenes.

### 9.3 Image treatment

- Preserve natural skin tones and material colors.
- Use gentle warmth and consistent contrast; do not use heavy presets.
- Avoid decorative color overlays.
- Text over photography requires a controlled dark or light scrim and tested contrast.
- Keep faces and critical architecture out of responsive crop danger zones.
- Provide useful alt text that describes the decision-relevant content.

### 9.4 Video

- Use short, quiet atmospheric clips when they add real understanding.
- Six to twelve seconds is usually enough for a background loop.
- Autoplay video must be muted, have no essential audio, and respect reduced-motion preferences.
- Longer videos should have captions and a visible play control.
- Never autoplay audio.
- Do not let video delay the booking action or dominate mobile bandwidth.

## 10. Iconography and illustration

### 10.1 Icons

- Use one consistent line-icon family. Lucide may remain when appropriate.
- Default size: 20–24 px.
- Icons should clarify an action or practical detail, not decorate every heading.
- Do not place every icon in its own colored rounded square.
- Pair icons with text labels; do not make unfamiliar icons carry meaning alone.
- Use custom icons only where Hi has a distinct concept that generic icons cannot represent.

### 10.2 Illustration

Illustration should be rare. Prefer real photography, simple diagrams, or the Hi mark.

Acceptable uses:

- a simple location map;
- a clear floor or seating diagram;
- a restrained line drawing for temporary unavailable imagery;
- bookstore or event artwork created as a genuine campaign asset.

Avoid generic startup illustrations, floating abstract shapes, and software onboarding art.

## 11. Spatial system and page composition

### 11.1 Page rhythm

| Element | Desktop | Mobile |
|---|---:|---:|
| Page gutter | 48–72 px | 20–24 px |
| Major section spacing | 96–128 px | 64–80 px |
| Minor section spacing | 48–64 px | 36–48 px |
| Content max width | 1200–1280 px | Fluid |
| Prose max width | 680 px | Fluid |

### 11.2 Composition rules

- Favor one strong column or a balanced two-column editorial split.
- Use full-bleed photography to create pace.
- Use a maximum of three repeated cards across on desktop; one on mobile.
- Alternate image-led and text-led sections rather than stacking boxes.
- Use whitespace before borders.
- Use hairline dividers for lists and schedules.
- Let images reach the edge of a composition where appropriate.
- Do not center every section. Alternate alignment to create editorial rhythm.
- Keep practical information close to the related action.

### 11.3 Corners, borders, and shadows

- Controls: 8 px radius.
- Ordinary surfaces: 8–12 px radius.
- Drawers and major overlays: up to 16 px radius.
- Full-bleed image fields may have square corners.
- Pills are reserved for tags, filters, and compact status—not every button.
- Borders are 1 px and low contrast.
- Use 2 px only for keyboard focus or a deliberate selected state.
- Ordinary content blocks should not have both a border and a shadow.
- Shadows are reserved for genuinely floating layers: navigation over an image, menus, dialogs, and drawers.

### 11.4 Preferred section patterns

Use these patterns repeatedly:

1. **Editorial hero** — real image, short statement, one sentence, one or two actions.
2. **Image/text split** — one visual, one concise explanation, one action.
3. **Practical information band** — hours, parking, Wi-Fi, access, and location in a single open row.
4. **Service row** — image, service name, short benefit, price or availability, action.
5. **Gallery sequence** — two to five images with minimal captions.
6. **Quote or member voice** — one strong statement with a real person or context.
7. **Event poster** — image-led event information with a human date and RSVP.
8. **Book feature** — cover-led editorial presentation.
9. **Open list** — simple rows separated by hairlines instead of cards.
10. **Focused conversion field** — one action area with no competing content.

## 12. Navigation and shell

The public shell must behave like a place and hospitality website, not an application header.

### 12.1 Primary public navigation

Use the approved customer-facing structure:

- **Work Here**
- **Book a Space**
- **Gather**
- **Bookstore**
- **Community**

Home is reached through the logo.

### 12.2 Utility actions

- **Book** should be the primary utility action.
- **Log in** or the account avatar should remain visually secondary.
- Contact, directions, policies, and social links belong in the footer or contextually within pages.

### 12.3 Authenticated navigation

Do not mix public navigation, member workflows, staff tools, and administrative tools in one horizontal bar.

- The public navigation remains stable after login.
- Member actions live in a simple account menu or member home.
- Staff and administrative tools live behind a clearly separate portal route or account-menu entry.
- Never show role names such as “Admin,” “Master,” or “Staff” as colorful public-navigation categories.
- Role and system state are operational details, not brand content.

### 12.4 Header treatment

Preferred options:

- Warm White or Hi Paper header with Hi Ink text;
- transparent header over a controlled hero image, becoming solid on scroll;
- thin divider or very soft floating shadow only when necessary.

Avoid a persistent dark SaaS-style application bar on public pages.

### 12.5 Mobile navigation

- Use a full-height or near-full-height menu panel.
- Keep the five public destinations visible without nested complexity.
- Put the booking action near the top or bottom thumb zone.
- Keep account and staff utilities in a separate quiet section.
- Use large tap targets and plain labels.

### 12.6 Footer

The footer should feel like the end of a visit, not a sitemap dump.

Include:

- logo and one-sentence promise;
- address and directions;
- hours;
- booking/contact action;
- essential page groups;
- newsletter or event updates only when operational;
- legal links;
- social links.

Do not repeat every authenticated or administrative route.

## 13. Page-family guidance

### 13.1 Home

Recommended sequence:

1. Real-space hero with **“Big ideas. Intimate space.”**
2. One concise explanation of Hi as a neighborhood workspace.
3. Primary action: **Book a space**; secondary action: **See how it works** or **View options**.
4. Three clear ways to work: coworking, day pass/drop-in, meeting room.
5. Image-led story of the space and why the intimate scale matters.
6. Practical information band: location, hours, parking, Wi-Fi, access.
7. Current event or community moment.
8. Bookstore feature.
9. Local member/community story.
10. Final booking action.

Do not use a “Who it is for” icon-card wall. Show those people through images and short narrative instead.

### 13.2 Work Here

Each service page should include:

- a truthful hero image;
- one-sentence explanation;
- price or starting price where known;
- what is included;
- who it works well for, expressed in ordinary language;
- a small gallery;
- availability or hours;
- a specific booking or inquiry action;
- a short FAQ only when necessary.

Do not present services as software tiers with feature comparison matrices unless genuine decision complexity requires it.

### 13.3 Book a Space

Booking should feel like a concierge-assisted decision, even when fully self-service.

Recommended flow:

1. What do you need: a seat or a room?
2. Choose date and time.
3. Choose the available option.
4. Review practical details and price.
5. Enter only the information needed.
6. Pay or confirm.
7. Receive a clear arrival summary.

Rules:

- Ask one meaningful question at a time.
- Use a subtle **Step 2 of 5**, not a complex progress dashboard.
- Show real room/seat imagery when choosing.
- Use human availability language.
- Keep the running summary concise.
- Do not show internal booking states, resource IDs, provider names, or payment implementation details.

### 13.4 Gather

Events should be visual and social.

Event listing:

- image or poster first;
- title;
- human date and time;
- one-line description;
- clear RSVP or ticket action.

Event detail:

- event image;
- concise promise;
- date, time, place, price, and availability;
- host identity;
- short description;
- accessibility or arrival details where relevant;
- RSVP.

Use a calendar grid only when event volume justifies it. Otherwise, an open chronological list is easier and more human.

### 13.5 Bookstore

The bookstore should feel editorial and tactile.

- Lead with book covers and photography.
- Treat Featured, Hi-owned titles, local/member authors, and curated books as editorial shelves.
- Keep title, author, price, one sentence, and purchase action visible.
- Do not display raw catalog metadata.
- Use categories quietly; do not turn the bookstore into an admin catalog.
- Product detail should include cover, summary, author, format, price, and purchase information without excessive boxes.

### 13.6 Community

Community should be shown through people, stories, events, local authors, partners, and useful local connections—not through abstract claims or member counts.

Good patterns:

- one member or local-business story at a time;
- a photo essay from an event;
- a local author feature;
- a simple partner line;
- a clear explanation of why Hi exists in Carrollton.

Avoid dense public directories, role labels, and profile-card grids unless the user intentionally enters a directory experience.

### 13.7 Member home

A member home should remain calm and place-oriented.

Prioritize:

- next booking or access information;
- one-tap booking;
- upcoming Hi events;
- membership inclusions;
- messages that require action;
- account and billing links.

Do not default to analytics, charts, system health, or a wall of summary cards.

### 13.8 Staff and administration

Staff and administrative tools may use denser tables, filters, and operational controls. They must still use the core palette, typography, accessible controls, and plain language.

However:

- operational density must not leak into public pages;
- staff/admin navigation should be separate;
- internal states may be visible only where staff need them;
- destructive and permission-sensitive actions require clear confirmation;
- public brand components must not be compromised to make administration easier.

## 14. Components

### 14.1 Buttons

**Primary:** Hi Forest background, Warm White text  
**Secondary:** transparent or Warm White, Hi Ink text, quiet hairline  
**Text action:** Hi Forest text with underline or arrow where useful  
**Destructive:** Error color, used only where necessary

Rules:

- Use 8 px radius by default.
- Minimum target size: 44 × 44 px.
- Labels begin with a clear verb.
- A section should normally have one filled button.
- Avoid all-pill controls.
- Avoid decorative shadows.
- Loading labels should describe the action: **Booking…**, **Saving…**, **Sending…**.

### 14.2 Cards

A card is appropriate only when the content is a genuinely discrete object, such as a book, event, room, or membership option.

Public card rules:

- image should occupy roughly half or more of the visual weight when one exists;
- title: no more than two lines;
- description: usually no more than two lines;
- one primary action;
- no more than one secondary metadata row;
- one border or one shadow, not both;
- no nested cards;
- avoid icon tiles, badges, and multiple labels in the same card;
- use 8–12 px radius, not oversized 24–32 px corners.

### 14.3 Badges and tags

Use badges only for information that benefits from compact scanning:

- Sold out
- A few seats left
- Members
- Free
- New
- Coming soon

Do not use badges as decorative section labels or to expose operational status.

### 14.4 Lists

Use open lists with generous spacing and hairline dividers for:

- events;
- amenities;
- FAQs;
- policies;
- bookings;
- bookstore shelves;
- member messages.

A list does not need a card around every row.

### 14.5 Forms

- Use a single-column form unless fields are tightly related.
- Keep labels above fields.
- Use placeholders only as examples, never as the only label.
- Explain why sensitive information is needed.
- Use plain-language validation next to the field.
- Preserve entered data after an error.
- Do not ask for information Hi does not need.
- Prefer a short multi-step flow over one overwhelming form.
- Use clear final actions: **Confirm booking**, **Join the event**, **Send inquiry**.

### 14.6 Notices

Notices should be rare and concise.

- Confirmation: one short sentence plus next action.
- Warning: what happened and what the person can do.
- Error: plain explanation, preserved work, and recovery.
- Do not include exception names, HTTP codes, provider names, or raw server messages.

### 14.7 Maps

- The map is a practical wayfinding tool, not a technology showcase.
- Show the Hi location clearly.
- Provide an equivalent written address and directions link.
- Keep controls minimal.
- Use the Hi marker at a legible size.
- Do not expose coordinate values, map provider metadata, or operational layers.

### 14.8 Tables

Tables should be uncommon on public pages. Use them only when true side-by-side comparison improves a decision.

When used:

- keep columns few;
- use sentence-case labels;
- avoid internal codes;
- provide a mobile alternative;
- do not place the table inside multiple bordered containers.

## 15. Voice and writing

### 15.1 Voice

Hi Coworking sounds:

- welcoming;
- direct;
- specific;
- confident;
- useful;
- lightly warm;
- locally grounded.

Hi does not sound:

- corporate;
- overly clever;
- technical;
- bureaucratic;
- promotional without evidence;
- exclusive;
- verbose.

### 15.2 Writing rules

- Lead with what the person can do.
- Use concrete nouns and active verbs.
- Keep headings to roughly seven words or fewer.
- Keep hero supporting copy to approximately 20–35 words.
- Keep ordinary card descriptions to approximately 12–22 words.
- Use contractions naturally.
- Use sentence case.
- Explain unfamiliar coworking terms.
- Write dates and times as people say them.
- Name the actual place: Carrollton, Isle of Wight, or the relevant local area.
- Avoid exclamation marks unless the moment genuinely warrants one.

### 15.3 Replace internal language

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| Browse available inventory configurations | Choose a desk or room |
| Resource unavailable | This time is full |
| Service not operational | Coming soon, or hide it |
| Member entitlement | Included with membership |
| Activate booking | Confirm booking |
| Authentication required | Log in to continue |
| Provider unavailable | We couldn’t complete that right now |
| Capacity exhausted | Sold out |
| User profile | Your profile |
| Facility access instructions | How to get in |
| Operational hours | Hours |
| Configuration | Option |
| Consume credits | Use credits |
| Organization record | Business profile |
| Status: pending | We’re reviewing it |

### 15.4 Public metadata policy

Show information only when it helps a customer decide or act.

Usually useful:

- date;
- time;
- place;
- price;
- duration;
- what is included;
- availability;
- capacity when relevant;
- accessibility;
- cancellation terms.

Usually not public-facing:

- internal ID;
- record status;
- revision number;
- database timestamp;
- provider code;
- workflow stage;
- role claim;
- source system;
- exact operational state;
- internal ownership;
- audit information.

## 16. Motion and interaction

Motion should help the interface feel smooth, not make the brand feel digital.

### Use

- 150–250 ms transitions;
- subtle opacity and position changes;
- one coordinated entrance rather than every item animating separately;
- image transitions that preserve context;
- clear hover and pressed states;
- smooth drawer and dialog movement.

### Avoid

- parallax;
- floating gradient objects;
- continuous pulsing;
- bouncing CTAs;
- excessive scroll-triggered animation;
- animated counters without decision value;
- shimmer loading across large public surfaces;
- motion that shifts the user’s target.

Always respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.

---

## 17. Accessibility

Hi Coworking should meet WCAG 2.2 AA across public and authenticated experiences.

Minimum requirements:

- normal text contrast of at least 4.5:1;
- large text contrast of at least 3:1;
- keyboard access to every action;
- visible 2 px focus indicator with offset;
- minimum 44 × 44 px touch targets;
- logical heading order;
- useful alt text;
- captions for meaningful video/audio;
- labels for all form controls;
- errors connected to the relevant field;
- no information communicated by color alone;
- reduced-motion support;
- dialogs that manage focus and can be dismissed;
- responsive text without horizontal scrolling at 320 px;
- no hover-only essential information.

Accessibility is part of hospitality. A person should not have to understand the implementation to use the product.

## 18. Responsive behavior

Hi Coworking is mobile-first because many people will discover, book, find directions, and check access details while away from a desk.

### Mobile priorities

1. What is this place?
2. Is it near me?
3. What can I book?
4. How much does it cost?
5. Is it available?
6. What do I do next?

### Rules

- Use one-column composition by default.
- Keep the primary action in easy thumb reach.
- Do not compress desktop card grids into tiny cards.
- Crop images deliberately for mobile.
- Keep price, date, location, and action together.
- Avoid horizontally scrolling tab systems for primary navigation.
- Use bottom sheets only when they simplify a focused task.
- Preserve browser back behavior and entered form data.
- Test at 320, 360, 390, 430, 768, 1024, 1280, and 1440 px widths.

## 20. Migration from the current interface

The following sequence will move the current product toward this guide without requiring a single uncontrolled redesign.

### Phase 1 — Establish the visual foundation

- Load Newsreader and DM Sans.
- Add semantic Hi color, spacing, radius, border, shadow, and motion tokens.
- Replace the cool slate page background with Hi Paper.
- Replace public indigo and bright emerald usage with Hi Forest and the supporting palette.
- Preserve the current Hi mark and primary line.

### Phase 2 — Separate place from application

- Redesign the public header around Work Here, Book a Space, Gather, Bookstore, and Community.
- Move member, staff, and administrative tools into separate account/portal navigation.
- Remove role-colored public links.
- Keep the booking action primary and login secondary.

### Phase 3 — Replace software presentation patterns

- Remove decorative background gradients and blur panels.
- Replace homepage icon-card grids with photography and editorial splits.
- Reduce oversized radii and shadows.
- Remove badges that function as headings.
- Replace nested containers with whitespace and dividers.

### Phase 4 — Build the image library

- Photograph the exterior, arrival, workstations, meeting space, books, events, materials, parking, and neighborhood context.
- Create desktop and mobile crops.
- Replace generic visual placeholders.
- Add accessible alt text and image-performance treatment.

### Phase 5 — Rewrite public language

- Remove internal and operational terms.
- Shorten section copy.
- Use specific actions.
- Hide inactive services.
- Make price, hours, parking, location, and availability easy to find.

### Phase 6 — Converge journeys

- Rebuild booking as a focused step-by-step flow.
- Make events poster-led and human.
- Make the bookstore cover-led and editorial.
- Make the member home calm and action-oriented.
- Apply the same public quality to login, registration, payment return, confirmation, and error states.

### Phase 7 — Validate

- Complete the acceptance checklist below.
- Test mobile first.
- Test keyboard, screen reader, reduced motion, zoom, contrast, and failure states.
- Compare the result against real hospitality/coworking sites, not SaaS dashboards.

## 21. Acceptance checklist

A public-facing page is not brand-complete until the answer to each applicable question is yes.

### Immediate comprehension

- Can a first-time visitor understand what Hi Coworking is within three seconds?
- Is the location or local context clear?
- Is the next action obvious?
- Is the page led by the place, not the platform?

### Visual character

- Does real imagery carry meaningful visual weight?
- Does the page feel warm, calm, and grounded?
- Is there enough open space?
- Are there no nested cards?
- Are borders and shadows used sparingly?
- Are there no decorative gradient blobs or glass panels?
- Could the page live comfortably beside a boutique hotel, neighborhood club, independent bookstore, or design-forward coworking brand?
- Could the page be mistaken for B2B SaaS? If yes, it fails.

### Content

- Is the copy short enough to scan?
- Are headings in ordinary language?
- Are actions specific?
- Are internal terms, IDs, roles, and workflow states absent?
- Is unavailable functionality hidden or described honestly?
- Are practical details easy to find?

### Components

- Is there only one primary action in each major region?
- Does every card represent a genuinely discrete object?
- Are image-led cards visually led by the image?
- Are buttons, fields, and tap targets large enough?
- Are notices written in human language?

### Journey

- Can the user complete the main task without understanding the system?
- Does mobile feel designed rather than compressed?
- Are price, availability, and expectations clear before commitment?
- Does confirmation explain what happens next?
- Are recovery paths clear when something fails?

### Accessibility and quality

- Does contrast meet WCAG AA?
- Can the page be completed by keyboard?
- Is focus visible?
- Do images and video have appropriate alternatives?
- Does reduced motion work?
- Is there no horizontal overflow at 320 px?
- Are loading, empty, error, and offline states truthful and usable?

## 23. Stewardship

This guide is the shared contract for Hi Coworking’s public-facing expression.

- New public components should use the established tokens and patterns.
- Exceptions should solve a clear customer need, not a developer convenience.
- A new visual pattern should be added only when an existing pattern cannot serve the need.
- Marketing, events, bookstore, booking, member experience, and physical signage should feel like one brand.
- The public experience has authority over the visual direction; administrative density must not redefine it.
- Changes to the brand line, primary mark, core palette, type families, or public-navigation model require explicit design review.

The final test is simple:

> **Does this help a person feel the place, understand the offer, and take the next step—without seeing the machinery behind it?**

If not, simplify it.
